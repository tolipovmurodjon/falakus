Earth's surface keeps changing — water, fields, floods, ground — but clouds and darkness hide most of it from ordinary satellite cameras. Falakus makes these changes visible with NISAR, the new NASA-ISRO radar satellite, which sees through cloud and at night, every 12 days.

We started at home. 10.9 million people in Uzbekistan's Fergana valley depend on reservoirs that cameras often cannot see: 15 of 24 winter photos in 2025 were mostly cloud. Our machine-learning model learned to find water in the radar and was tested on days it had never seen; it beat the standard method (map overlap 0.63 vs 0.59), and NASA's ICESat-2 laser confirmed it (correlation 0.95). We show the reservoir losing half its water to cotton fields, the harvest moving field by field, and the Aral Sea gone since 1997.

Then we opened it to the world: a map of every NISAR pass, flood watch on NASA's event feed, ground motion from an earthquake measured in centimetres, ship tracking, rain and wildfire layers, and a 40-year time-lapse.

Every number sits next to an outside check, and every change comes with a plain-language briefing: what happened, why, and what comes next.
