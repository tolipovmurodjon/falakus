About two-thirds of Earth is under cloud at any moment, and half of every day is night. So the changes that shape people's lives, from shrinking reservoirs and floods to harvests and sinking ground, often happen where satellite cameras cannot see.

Falakus makes them visible with NISAR, the new NASA-ISRO radar satellite. It sends its own signal, so it sees through cloud and at night, across the whole planet, every 12 days. Calm water turns black, so we track reservoirs and floods under storm clouds. Leaves twist the radar wave, so we see harvests field by field. The wave's timing measures ground motion in centimetres after an earthquake. Ships shine bright on dark seas, day or night.

We proved it at home first. In Uzbekistan, 10.9 million people depend on reservoirs that cameras lost to cloud in 15 of 24 winter photos. Our machine-learning model, tested on days it never saw, mapped the water better than the standard method (0.63 vs 0.59 overlap), and NASA's ICESat-2 laser confirmed it (0.95 correlation).

Anyone can explore every NISAR pass worldwide, NASA flood and fire events, and a 40-year time-lapse, with plain briefings on what changed, why, and what comes next.
