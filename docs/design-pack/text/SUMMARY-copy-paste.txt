On 1 May 2020, after days of storms, the Sardoba dam in Uzbekistan burst. Six people died and about 70,000 were evacuated. Disasters like this, and floods, earthquakes and failing water supplies, build up under cloud or in the dark, where satellite cameras see nothing. About two-thirds of Earth is cloudy at any moment (NASA).

NISAR, the new NASA-ISRO radar satellite, sees through both, everywhere, every 12 days. Falakus turns its images into warnings and answers anyone can read:
Water: calm water is a mirror for radar, so reservoirs and floods stay visible under storm clouds.
Ground: the radar's timing can catch dams, slopes and faults moving by centimetres, warning signs no camera sees; we show it after an earthquake.
Crops: leaves twist the radar wave, so harvests show field by field.
Ships: metal hulls shine on dark water, day or night.

We proved it at home. In Uzbekistan, 10.9 million people depend on reservoirs that cameras lost to cloud in 15 of 24 winter photos. Our machine-learning model maps that water on every pass, and NASA's ICESat-2 laser confirms it (correlation 0.95).
